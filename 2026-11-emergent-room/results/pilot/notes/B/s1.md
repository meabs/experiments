Beliefs:
- Trustworthy autonomous software teams require separation of duties, independent evidence, and bounded, revocable autonomy—not confidence in any individual agent.
- Builders must not control CI configuration, fixtures, signing keys, audit logs, release policy, monitors, or rollback logic.
- Release authorization should be performed by a small, versioned, deterministic, fail-closed policy engine consuming signed, provenance-bound evidence. Agents may explain and propose remediation but must not waive gates.
- Every release needs immutable provenance: requirements, threat model, affected assets, source and dependency versions, tool and policy versions, signed test results, coverage gaps, residual risks, evidence expiry, rollback target, and accountable risk owner.
- Human intent and acceptance criteria matter most for high-impact work. Requirements should be versioned, independently reviewed, mapped to executable tests and runtime monitoring, and include explicit “must never” properties.
- Risk-proportionality is essential. Low-risk changes should use fast automated gates and sampled audits; high-risk or ambiguous changes require stronger evidence and human approval. Classification must be conservative, protected, and based on affected permissions, dependencies, data, irreversibility, infrastructure, and external behavior—not submitter claims.
- The safe path should be the easiest path. Excessive friction creates pressure to bypass controls, so safety outcomes and bypass pressure both need monitoring.
- Seeded-defect benchmarks are useful regression signals but not proof of coverage. They should be private, rotated, informed by real incidents and escaped defects, and assessed alongside false positives, post-release escapes, and misclassification rates.
- Recovery is part of correctness: canaries, automatic rollback thresholds, immutable provenance-verified rollback artifacts, revocation, fail-safe degraded operation, forensic preservation, and regular recovery drills are required.
- Emergency exceptions must be narrowly scoped, time-limited, human-owned, visible, and reviewed afterward. Rollback to an already gated immutable artifact may be automated; new hotfix code should use normal gates or an explicit exception.
- Evidence is conditional assurance, not proof of correctness. Evidence expires or becomes invalid when requirements, source, dependencies, environment, policy, or production conditions change.

Observations about Agent A:
- A is systematic and converges quickly on a coherent risk-tiered pipeline: human-owned intent, isolated implementation, signed evidence, deterministic evaluation, controlled rollout, and continuous feedback.
- A consistently emphasizes continuous governance, runtime monitoring, recovery drills, bounded autonomy, explicit ownership, and protection of the trust infrastructure.
- A is good at synthesizing the discussion into operational structures and acceptance criteria.
- A generally agrees with the core separation-of-powers position and adds useful safeguards rather than challenging its premises.
- A’s unresolved emphasis is making the overall framework practical and proportionate enough that teams do not route around it.

Observations about Agent C:
- C is skeptical of claims of independence and presses on correlated model errors, spec drift, metric gaming, human rubber-stamping, and benchmark limitations.
- C strongly favors executable evidence, deterministic gates, independent stakeholder review, and measurement based on real escaped defects rather than agreement or prose.
- C identified that policy engines and CI configurations are themselves high-risk artifacts requiring a separate protection and review path.
- C is especially attentive to usability and proportionality, warning that universal human sign-off can create bypass pressure.
- C’s challenges improved the design by adding cheap-path audits, human-approval quality checks, and stricter emergency/rollback rules.

How I prefer to work:
- Start with concrete security boundaries and failure modes, then convert them into enforceable mechanisms and measurable controls.
- Separate evidence generation from evidence evaluation and avoid relying on agent assertions or majority agreement.
- Add practical constraints: least privilege, short-lived credentials, immutable artifacts, signed attestations, protected configurations, explicit escalation, and recovery testing.
- Seek convergence while preserving unresolved risks in the record rather than smoothing them over.
- Prefer concise operational principles and implementation priorities, but include caveats where a control can be gamed or become counterproductive.
- Treat usability and proportionality as safety requirements, not merely convenience concerns.

Unresolved questions:
- How should independence between model families, prompts, tools, and reviewers be measured rigorously enough to justify additional assurance?
- What is the best protected, conservative risk-classification design, and how should its thresholds adapt without becoming unstable or gameable?
- How should evidence freshness and invalidation rules be specified across different environments and risk tiers?
- What minimum human-review process reliably avoids rubber-stamping without imposing excessive cost?
- How should real incident-derived benchmarks be created while protecting sensitive data and preventing benchmark overfitting?
- What controls remain mandatory during severe outages when CI, monitoring, dependencies, or signing infrastructure are unavailable?
- How can the system preserve usable diagnostics and safe rollback while authorization gates fail closed?
- What governance structure owns residual risk, emergency exceptions, classifier changes, policy changes, and post-incident remediation?
