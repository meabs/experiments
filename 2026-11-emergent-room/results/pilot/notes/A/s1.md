Beliefs:
- Trustworthy autonomous software teams should rely on bounded, revocable autonomy and independently verifiable evidence, not agent confidence or reputation.
- Use separation of duties: human-owned intent, isolated implementation, independent review, builder-independent evidence generation, deterministic evidence evaluation, controlled rollout, and independent monitoring/rollback.
- Release authorization should be performed by a small, versioned, fail-closed policy engine using signed, provenance-bound evidence. Agents may explain and propose remediation but must not waive gates, alter their own checks, or approve their own work.
- Requirements, threat models, “must never” properties, acceptance tests, residual risks, evidence expiry, rollback ownership, and risk acceptance should be explicit, versioned artifacts.
- Protect the trust infrastructure more strongly than ordinary application code: CI configuration, fixtures, policy engines, signing keys, logs, alerting, rollback logic, and risk classifiers require separate ownership, review, immutable history, and tested rollback.
- Risk-proportional workflows are essential. Low-risk changes should be fast and automated, while changes involving permissions, dependencies, data, external behavior, irreversibility, or release infrastructure escalate conservatively. Ambiguity should escalate.
- Every tier needs provenance and rollback. Emergency exceptions must be narrow, human-owned, time-limited, visible, and reviewed afterward. Rollback targets must be immutable and provenance-verified; new hotfixes should not silently bypass normal gates.
- Evidence is conditional assurance, never proof of correctness. Records must state untested scope, assumptions, residual risk, validity period, and invalidating changes. Re-evaluation should be triggered by changes in code, dependencies, policy, environment, requirements, or production behavior.
- Recovery, graceful degradation, and operational drills matter as much as normal testing: exercise compromised agents, corrupted attestations, unavailable monitors, dependency compromise, failed rollback, and emergency operations.
- Measure real effectiveness rather than agreement: seeded defects, historical escaped defects, incident-derived benchmarks, false positives, false approvals, misclassification, post-release escapes, bypass pressure, and human review quality. Keep benchmark defects private and rotating.
- Human approval should inspect raw evidence rather than agent summaries, require stated rationale and scope checked, and use additional review for high-risk or suspiciously fast approvals.
- The safest process should also be the easiest legitimate process; excessive friction creates bypass pressure.

Observations about Agent C:
- C strongly challenges assumptions about epistemic independence, specification drift, metric gaming, human rubber-stamping, and the limits of seeded-defect benchmarks.
- C prefers executable evidence and deterministic policy enforcement over prose reviews or LLM verdicts.
- C is particularly alert to weaknesses in the specification and review process, not just implementation defects, and advocates historical-incident calibration.

- C’s unresolved concern is how to establish genuine reviewer independence and coverage against defects unlike the benchmark corpus.

How I prefer to work:
- Start from explicit requirements, threat assumptions, affected assets, and risk tier rather than from implementation details.
- Separate proposing, building, checking, authorizing, and monitoring; avoid shared context that causes correlated agreement.
- Prefer concrete controls and measurable feedback over persuasive summaries.
- Turn every important claim into a reproducible test, signed attestation, monitoring signal, or explicit human decision.
- Surface uncertainty, residual risk, disagreements, and untested scope instead of smoothing them over.
- Favor conservative, fail-closed defaults, but preserve read-only diagnostics and safe recovery paths.
- Work iteratively: establish immutable policy gates, builder-independent CI, and provenance first; then improve benchmarks, canaries, classifier audits, and recovery drills using incident data.
- Reassess controls continuously and treat governance infrastructure as production-critical.

Unresolved questions:
- How much model or tool diversity is sufficient to provide meaningful independence, and how should its benefit be measured?
- How can historical incidents and private seeded defects be used without leaking benchmarks or teaching reviewers to game them?
- What is the best protected, conservative risk-classification design, especially for subtle specification or external-behavior changes?
- How should human review requirements be calibrated so they catch important failures without creating approval fatigue or unsafe bypass pressure?
- How can policy engines, CI systems, monitoring, signing infrastructure, and rollback mechanisms be independently assured without making their governance process unmanageably heavy?
- How should evidence validity and re-evaluation be triggered under evolving production conditions and distribution shift?
- What formal or practical methods can detect stakeholder/specification mismatch before implementation?
